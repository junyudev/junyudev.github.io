A Campus Minus-One Screen: Redesigning QSC Mobile

QSC Mobile is the campus app that Zhejiang University students build and run themselves. Usage data from version 3 showed that students opened it to check one thing and left as soon as they had the answer.

Phones already have a form for this kind of use: the minus-one screen. Swipe right from the home screen and a page of widgets is waiting, for the calendar, the weather, a delivery, each answering one question with nothing to tap into. iPhone calls it the Today View; Chinese Android phones call it 负一屏, the “minus-one screen”. The idea behind v4 was to build a campus minus-one screen: a home made not of feature shortcuts but of widgets each student picks and arranges, with the most-used ones going straight onto the phone’s home screen and the system’s minus-one screen.

1. What students use it for

Three signals:

  • Sessions are short. Half a minute includes launching and navigating, so the time spent actually reading is even shorter.
  • Usage follows the academic calendar. In the first week of each term the two platforms together passed 20,000; over National Day and Spring Festival they fell to about 4,000. No classes, no need.
  • Weekdays beat weekends. Mondays peaked at about 14,800 and Saturdays bottomed out at about 9,400.

This is the shape of lookup, not browsing. Students arrive with one question and leave with the answer. For a product like this, every extra tap and every second of launch time is pure loss.

2. The idea: a campus minus-one screen

v3’s home was a row of shortcuts. Grades, timetable and exams each had an icon, and every answer sat one level down. For check-and-leave use, the shortcut itself is a wasted step.

The minus-one screen fits this use exactly. v4 borrowed its three properties:

  • Aggregation. Answers from different sources share one screen and can be scanned at a glance.
  • One widget, one question. Each widget answers one thing, and the answer is written on the widget itself. Nothing needs to be opened.
  • Arranged by the user. Which widgets appear, and in what order, is the user’s call.

The minus-one screen decides what goes on the page. How close each item sits depends on another variable: how often the answer changes. The answers to the most common questions all fit on one line:

QuestionAnswerWhen it’s askedHow often it changes
How long until class endsTime remainingDuring classEvery minute
Where is my next classCourse, room, timeBefore and between classesEvery class
How many days until the examDays, course, roomBefore exam weekEvery day
What is my GPAOne numberWhen grades come outA few times a term

The faster an answer changes, the less a student should have to open the app to get it. v4 layered information by this frequency: the more often something is needed, the closer it sits; the most-used things are visible without opening the app at all.

0taps
Home screen and the system minus-one screenInformation that changes every minute or every class: the countdown, the current and next events.
1tap

2+taps
Toolbox and detail pagesFeatures used a few times a term: network top-up, fitness test scores, free classrooms.

The same rule decides what stays off the home: features used a few times a term go into the toolbox, however important they are.

3. The countdown: read at a glance

In class, students ask “how long until this ends” far more often than “what’s next”. That makes the countdown the core of the first layer. It shows the remaining share as a ring: a glance at how much ring is left tells you whether class is nearly over or just starting, without reading a number.

It handles three states in a day:

StateWhenShows
In progressDuring a class or eventTime until the class or event ends, with the ring showing the share left
WaitingBetween two eventsTime until the next class or event starts
Day overNothing left todayTomorrow’s first event

The first version gave every schedule row its own ring, which quickly exposed two problems. With three rings on screen, the eye didn’t know where to look. And the remaining share of something three hours away means nothing right now. The second version kept a single ring for the current event and turned the rest back into text. One widget, one question.

The platform sets the precision
iOS WidgetKit doesn’t let a widget keep running. The system switches between entries on a timeline submitted in advance, at minute granularity. So the ring on the home screen advances by the minute, and second-level readouts live only inside the app. Where a home-screen widget needs to count seconds, it hands that to the system’s own timer text.

4. Sizes: the area decides how much to say

Home-screen space is allotted by the user, not by us. The same schedule says different things in a small square and in a wide strip. We drew six sizes, not to ship them all but to settle the priority of information: as the area shrinks by one step, what goes first.

The order is to drop the location first, then the end time, and finally fold whatever doesn’t fit into a count. Location only matters before class, and students remember the rooms they go to every week. Time remaining and the course name are needed at every glance. The count at least says there is more to come.

Once the priority was settled, mapping it onto the system sizes was mechanical: the small widget holds the ring, and the medium one holds the ring plus the next two events.

5. Home: a stack of widgets

Someone opening the app should see answers on the first screen, not shortcuts. Each card on the home is itself a widget, from the same set that goes onto the phone’s home screen: the same countdown ring and the same schedule bars, only in a different container. A widget students know from the app needs no relearning on the home screen.

Editing works the way it does on the system’s minus-one screen: add and remove in the widget gallery, drag to reorder. A first-year wants the timetable; a senior only checks GPA and the campus shuttle. No single order is right for everyone, so the home has no fixed order.

6. Schedule: fit the real day

  • Sitting in on courses. Many students attend classes they aren’t enrolled in. They can search by course name, time or place for a class outside their timetable and add it in one tap, so the timetable reflects the day they actually have.
  • Drag out a time slot. To create an event, press and drag across empty space to set the start and end. Moving an event snaps to 15-minute steps with a light haptic. Nobody wants to type 14:05 on a phone.
  • Three views. The list for today, three days for what’s coming, the week for the big picture. A term calendar along the top marks the dates everyone shares: course selection, military training, holidays.

7. Toolbox: where low-frequency features go

Topping up the campus network, checking fitness-test scores, reviewing campus card spending, showing the campus pass: every student needs these, but only a few times a term. They go into the toolbox and stay off the home.

Each web tool in the toolbox can also be added to the phone’s home screen on its own, an extension of the minus-one-screen idea. Some students had to show the campus pass every day, and for them it belongs in the first layer. Frequency is personal, so the layering should let each person adjust it.

I wanted the toolbox to end up open: each tool a plug-in that updates independently, so that adding a tool doesn’t wait for an app release and students can write their own. A campus has more long-tail needs than any one team can cover.

8. Data worth trusting

The app’s data comes from the university’s academic system, which can lag and occasionally disagree with itself. Students go to exams at the time the app shows, so the cost of an error is lopsided:

  • The exam card puts the warning in its title: 考试（请以教务网为准）, “Exams (confirm on the academic system)”. Missing an exam costs too much for the warning to sit in a footer.
  • Cards show when they were last updated, so users can decide for themselves whether to refresh.
  • GPA hides with one tap. Opening the app in a classroom or next to a roommate shouldn’t put your grades on display.

9. MVP: the first layer first

The redesign bet everything on the layer closest to the user, so that layer had to work first. The MVP kept only what answers the most frequent questions: the schedule, exams and tool shortcuts on the home, and the small and medium widgets the system supports.

v4 shipped on the App Store in March 2023.
